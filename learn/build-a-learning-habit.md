      <h1>Learning Habit Kaise Banayein?</h1>
      <p class="article-lede">Continuous learning does not require hours of free time or perfect motivation. It needs a small repeatable loop: choose one idea, practise recalling it, apply it, and reflect on what to improve. Start small enough that you can return tomorrow.</p>
    </div>
  </header>

  <h2>Why learning habits often break</h2>
  <p>People often begin with a large goal—finish a course, master a tool, or read every day—but leave the next action vague. Busy days then turn into missed sessions, and missed sessions become a reason to stop. Make the action concrete, short, and easy to restart.</p>

  <h2>Build a 15-minute learning loop</h2>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Choose one question (2 minutes):</strong> name the specific thing you want to understand. For example, “When should I use an AI agent instead of a simple chatbot?”</li>
    <li><strong>Learn one small idea (5 minutes):</strong> read a short section or study one example. Avoid collecting resources without engaging with them.</li>
    <li><strong>Recall it from memory (3 minutes):</strong> close the source and explain the idea in your own words. Note what you could not explain clearly.</li>
    <li><strong>Apply it (3 minutes):</strong> solve a tiny problem, make a comparison, or teach the idea to someone else.</li>
    <li><strong>Reflect and choose the next step (2 minutes):</strong> write one takeaway and one question to revisit.</li>
  </ol>

  <h2>Use cues, not motivation alone</h2>
  <p>Attach the routine to something that already happens: after your morning tea, before the first meeting, or just after lunch. Keep the learning material ready and decide the next session's question before you stop. Reduce friction instead of relying on willpower.</p>

  <h2>Make learning active</h2>
  <p>Re-reading can feel comfortable while hiding gaps in understanding. Add active recall: explain the idea without notes, answer a question, or recreate the steps. Return to important ideas after a delay, and apply them in more than one situation. You do not need a complex tracking system; a short note with the question, answer, and next action is enough.</p>

  <h2>Run a one-week experiment</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Days 1–2:</strong> pick a stable cue and complete a 10–15 minute session.</li>
    <li><strong>Days 3–4:</strong> add a short recall test before checking your notes.</li>
    <li><strong>Days 5–6:</strong> apply one idea to a real task or explain it to another person.</li>
    <li><strong>Day 7:</strong> review what you remember, what you used, and what made the routine easy or difficult.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>Measure completed sessions and useful applications—not streaks alone. If you miss a day, restart with the smallest version of the habit.</p>

  <h2>Key takeaway</h2>
  <p>A learning habit becomes sustainable when the next action is clear, small, and connected to a real purpose. Learn one thing, recall it, apply it, and evolve the routine based on what you discover.</p>
